Provenance doesn't survive the trip

126 labeled tracks across three generators, 78 perturbation trials, and a benchmark against Meta's AudioSeal. Recall is 4.8% — and the reason reframes what the product has to be.

01

The headline

On a 126-file corpus spanning three generators, detection recall is 4.8% — 4 of 84 AI tracks. Not because the code is wrong. Precision and specificity are both 100%, and the four it catches it identifies perfectly, down to the content ID.

It is because provenance metadata does not survive redistribution. A single re-encode destroys it, and 95% of the AI corpus had already been through one.

What this changes

Reading C2PA is necessary, and it now works. But it only ever sees files fetched straight from the generator. Everything downstream of that needs a watermark. This is the strongest argument in the deck, and it is now measured rather than asserted.

02

Why recall collapsed

The survival harness takes files that are known to carry a signed Google manifest, re-encodes them the way an upload pipeline would, and re-reads them.

OperationSurvivedRateWhat it models
original 4/4 · 100% straight from the generator
mp3_320 0/4 · 0% any upload pipeline
mp3_192 0/4 · 0% streaming delivery
mp3_128 0/4 · 0% lossy redistribution
wav_bounce 0/4 · 0% a DAW export
id3_stripped 0/4 · 0% a one-line strip
filled = AI-generation claim still readablen = 4 Gemini tracks with signed manifests

Every operation destroys it — including 320 kbps, which is sonically transparent, and a lossless WAV bounce that changes no audio at all. C2PA lives in the file container, and re-encoding rebuilds the container.

That also explains the Suno and Udio result. Those tracks were re-encoded with LAME 3.100 by the dataset authors and carry no ID3 tag whatsoever.

Don't over-claim this one

Their 0% shows what survives collection and redistribution — not that Suno and Udio never disclosed. We are reading tracks that were already stripped before we got them. Whether those platforms disclose at generation time is still an open question, and it needs first-party downloads to answer.

The consequence is worth stating plainly: Google already does both — a C2PA manifest and an inaudible SynthID watermark. The manifest dies on transcode. The watermark survives. And we cannot read it, because Google does not release the detector.

03

Detection missed real C2PA manifests

Fixed

All four Gemini tracks carry a fully signed Google manifest. The disclosure sits in the file in plain terms:

c2pa.created "Created by Google Generative AI." digitalSourceType: …/trainedAlgorithmicMedia c2pa.edited "Applied imperceptible SynthID watermark." signed by Google LLC / Google Media Processing Services chaining to Google C2PA Root CA G3 validation Trusted (official C2PA trust list)

Before the fix, the tool reported these as “Unknown with metadata hints” — the strongest possible disclosure signal, on the exact statutory use case, scored as a near-miss.

scan_metadata searched for the string c2pa, then parsed what followed as JSON. Production C2PA is CBOR inside JUMBF boxes, carried in an ID3 GEOB frame. The tool understood only the manifests it writes itself — it could read its own output and nothing else.

The fix is a dependency-free JUMBF walker and CBOR decoder. It reads identity only; cryptographic trust stays c2patool's job, so an unvalidated manifest stays at claim level detected, never verified.

All three verdict policies score identically, because nothing lands in the intermediate claim levels — a file either has a readable manifest or it has nothing at all.

The earlier 100% figure was real, but it measured only Gemini: the one provider with a clean C2PA implementation. Widening to Suno and Udio dropped recall by 95 points. That is exactly why the first run's n=5 was labeled a smoke test rather than a validation.

05

The shipping decoder fails on MP3

Decision needed

Three carriers × 26 perturbations = 78 trials, scoring exact record-ID recovery. Every MP3 bitrate defeats the shipping decoder — including 320 kbps.

The watermark is not the problem. MP3 adds ~1800 samples of codec delay. The payload survives intact — sync search recovers the exact record ID at z ≈ 13 — but the shipping decoder assumes it starts at sample 0, looks in the wrong place, and reports z ≈ 1.2.

The fix is contained. A shift of d samples is q·BIT_LEN + r, so sweeping the sub-bit offset r over [0, BIT_LEN) and the payload phase q mod NBITS covers every possible misalignment. One FFT cross-correlation gets all offsets at once. Runtime is 0.07–0.28 s per 30 seconds of audio.

It is written and tested in eval/detectors.py, and deliberately not merged — changing the shipping decoder is the team's call, not a side effect of a validation pass.

One perturbation is genuinely fatal: a 1% tempo change rescales the bit grid itself.

Benchmarked against Meta AudioSeal

AudioSeal is the one third-party scheme we can measure end to end, because its detector is public — unlike SynthID's.

We are blind to AudioSeal. It ships in Meta's audio models and already sits in our provider catalog, so AudioSeal-watermarked audio is a live coverage gap. Its detector is open, so this one is fixable — at the cost of a torch dependency, which is a real question for a Vercel deployment.

Our watermark is ~16 dB louder than Meta's. That buys robustness — we beat AudioSeal on 20 dB SNR noise, where it drops to p = 0.51 — but 20 dB under the music is loud enough that nobody should call it imperceptible without a listening test.

07

Smaller findings

Vendor hints fire on random audio bytes Open

The metadata scan reads the whole file, audio payload included. Two false hits across the corpus — “Meta” on a Gemini track, “OpenAI” on an FMA human recording. Thirteen of 47 vendor aliases are five characters or fewer. Both stayed hints and never became positives, so precision held, but scoping the scan to metadata regions removes the class.

32-bit float WAVs bypass the primary reader

Stdlib wave rejects them; detection falls back to miniaudio and still works. The fallback is load-bearing for a format DAWs export by default.

A bug in our own harness, for the record

The first AudioSeal run showed MP3 destroying both watermarks. That was wrong. 192 kbps is illegal at 16 kHz, so LAME silently switched MPEG layers and resampled to 44.1 kHz — a 2.8× time-scale change, reported as codec damage. The perturbation now clamps to the rate's legal bitrate ladder and converts back. The 44.1 kHz results were never affected.
